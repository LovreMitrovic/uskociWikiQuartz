Sukošan

Sukošan roughly doubles between the two surveys — 54 households to 104, 328 souls to 726 — but only 13 of its 39 recorded 1610 surnames are still there in 1756. The village grew by replacement and in-migration rather than by natural increase of the 1610 families. The kin-groups that do persist are the ones that were already rooted: Smolić (3 households in 1610, 8 in 1756 — the village’s largest), Krečić, Milošević, Gracalić, Čičić, with Raspović expanding from one household to five and Marinović from one to four.

In both censuses (13): Smolić 3→8, Krečić 4→4, Milošević 3→4, Raspović 1→5, Gracalić 3→2, Marinović 1→4, Čičić 2→2, Letina 1→2, Matošić 1→2, Novoselić 2→1, Perić 1→2, Bičić 1→1, Vičinović 1→1.

How much of this turnover is real?

Some of it is certainly an artifact. The two censuses were written by different scribes 146 years apart, and their Venetian spellings were normalized into Croatian by two separate ingests using different judgement — so one family can enter the wiki under two hubs and read as a disappearance plus an arrival. The pairs listed above are the visible cases; there are probably more that no longer look similar enough to spot. Treat the continuity figures as a lower bound.

The rest is real, and has a cause: the Morean War (1684–1699) and the Venetian resettlement that followed it moved large numbers of people into the Zadar contado from Ottoman territory. Both effects push in the same direction, which is why the raw overlap is so low.
